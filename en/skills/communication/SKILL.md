# Communication and Presentation Frameworks

Based on the user's $ARGUMENTS, apply the appropriate framework from the list below.

**Framework selection guide**:
| Situation | Recommended framework |
|------|-------------------|
| Reports, emails, and Slack messages to your manager | PREP |
| Proposals and approval requests to executives | TAPS |
| Building the logic of a complex argument or a long report | Pyramid Structure |
| Introducing yourself or your business to investors or people you meet for the first time | Elevator Pitch |

## 01. PREP

**Purpose**: A speaking framework for communicating logically with the conclusion first. It is the most basic of all business communication tools, and it works in every situation (presentations, reports, emails, Slack, 1on1s).

**When to use it**:
- Reports and updates to your manager or clients
- Communicating requirements by email or chat
- Speaking up and stating opinions in meetings
- Answering questions in interviews

**Template**:
| Step | Content | Key point |
|---------|------|---------|
| **P**oint (conclusion) | | State it in one sentence. "We should..." or "We need..." |
| **R**eason (reason) | | One to three grounds that support the conclusion. "Because..." |
| **E**xample (concrete example) | | Reinforce the reason with numbers, facts, and experience. "In fact..." |
| **P**oint (restated conclusion) | | Repeat the opening conclusion in different words and call for action |

### Worked example

**Situation**: Proposing a reallocation of the marketing budget at the weekly team meeting

| Step | Content |
|---------|------|
| **P**oint (conclusion) | We should shift 30% of the Q3 digital advertising budget from search ads to social media ads. |
| **R**eason (reason) | The CPA (cost per acquisition) of social media ads is less than half that of search ads, so the same budget brings in more new customers. |
| **E**xample (concrete example) | In last month's A/B test, the CPA through social media ads was 1,200 yen, compared with 2,800 yen for search ads. In addition, customers acquired through social media ads tend to have a 15% higher LTV (lifetime value). |
| **P**oint (restated conclusion) | Therefore, let's shift budget to social media ads in Q3 and maximize new customer acquisition efficiency. I will deliver a concrete allocation plan by next Monday. |

### Common mistakes

1. **Vague conclusion**: Starting with a vague expression such as "It might be worth considering...". State the conclusion as "We should..." or "I propose...".
2. **Abstract example**: Instead of "Sales are growing", show numbers such as "120% year over year, reaching monthly revenue of 30 million yen". The Example should be concrete enough that the listener can picture it.
3. **Final Point is just a copy-paste**: Repeating exactly the same sentence as the opening leaves a weak impression. The final Point should include a call to action, such as "That is why we should..." or "As the next action, ...".

### Tips

- **The 30-second rule**: Keep PREP to a length you can say in 30 seconds to 1 minute. If it gets longer, consider Pyramid Structure.
- **At most three Reasons**: With four or more reasons, the listener cannot remember them. Narrow down to the one to three strongest reasons.
- **Use different forms for writing and speaking**: In email, show the structure with bullet points. When speaking, make the structure explicit with connectors such as "To start with the conclusion" and "There are two reasons".
- **Negative reports need PREP most**: The worse the news, the more you gain the other person's trust by stating the conclusion first.

### Next steps

- To further strengthen the grounds of a proposal delivered with PREP → **Pyramid Structure** (this skill, 03) to deepen the logical structure
- If the problem needs to be structured before the proposal → **problem** skill (Logic Tree, Issue Tree)
- To turn the proposal into an execution plan → **execution** skill (OKR, WBS, Gantt Chart)

## 02. TAPS

**Purpose**: A persuasion and proposal framework for moving people to act. It specializes in giving the audience a sense of ownership that "we must change" and prompting behavior change. Best for proposals to executives, internal approval requests, and improvement proposals to clients.

**When to use it**:
- New business proposals and improvement proposals to executives
- Consulting proposals to clients
- Writing internal approval documents
- Calling on team members to change

**Template**:
| Step | Content | Key point |
|---------|------|---------|
| **T**o be (ideal state) | | Paint an ideal the audience can relate to. A quantitative goal is even better |
| **A**s is (current state) | | Show the current state with objective data and facts. Do not get emotional |
| **P**roblem (problem) | | Make the gap between To be and As is clear. Create urgency: "If this continues..." |
| **S**olution (solution) | | Present concrete, feasible measures. Include cost, timeline, and expected effect |

### Worked example

**Situation**: Proposing digital transformation of customer support to the company president

| Step | Content |
|---------|------|
| **T**o be (ideal state) | A state where 80% of customer inquiries are resolved within 24 hours and customer satisfaction (NPS) is raised to +40 or higher, above the industry average. |
| **A**s is (current state) | The current average response time is 48 hours and NPS is +12. Even though 60% of inquiries are routine, such as "checking delivery status" and "return procedures", all of them are handled manually. The support team's overtime averages 35 hours per month. |
| **P**roblem (problem) | Routine inquiries are consuming human resources, and the quality of responses to complex issues is declining. If left as is, there is a risk of support collapse when the customer count grows 20% next fiscal year. Also, competitor Company A has already introduced a chatbot and is starting to pull ahead in response speed. |
| **S**olution (solution) | Introduce an AI chatbot and automate 70% of routine inquiries. Initial investment 8 million yen, monthly operating cost 300,000 yen. Implementation period 3 months. Expected effects: response time shortened from 48 hours to 4 hours, support team overtime reduced 50%, NPS of +30 or higher achieved within 6 months. ROI expected to be recovered in 12 months. |

### Common mistakes

1. **To be serves only your own interests**: Instead of "We want to double sales", paint an ideal that the audience (executives, customers) can agree "should be the case". Include the customer's and the market's perspective.
2. **As is has no data**: Instead of "It feels slow", quantify it as "average response time 48 hours". If there is no data, first propose measuring it.
3. **A leap between Problem and Solution**: Jumping to a solution with shallow problem analysis is not convincing. Dig one level into the cause-and-effect of "why is this problem occurring" before presenting the Solution.

### Tips

- **Start writing from To be**: Starting from As is (complaints about the current state) narrows your view. Paint the ideal first, then identify the gaps.
- **Match the audience's To be**: If the audience is the president, talk about To be in management metrics; for a frontline manager, in operational metrics; for an engineer, in technical metrics.
- **Prepare multiple Solutions**: Offering three tiers (good, better, best) makes the decision easier. Indicate the recommended option while still giving choices.
- **Put the supporting numbers in an appendix**: Keep the TAPS body to one A4 page and put the detailed data in a separate appendix.

### Next steps

- To structure the gap between To be and As is in depth → **problem** skill (As-is/To-be, Logic Tree)
- To test the feasibility of the Solution from the market side → **market** skill (PEST Analysis, Five Forces Analysis)
- To turn the Solution into a concrete execution plan → **execution** skill (WBS, RACI)
- To build the proposal's storyline logically → **Pyramid Structure** (this skill, 03)

## 03. Pyramid Structure

**Purpose**: Organize a claim and its grounds in a hierarchy to create logical, persuasive documents, presentations, and reports. The method was systematized by Barbara Minto of McKinsey and is the standard thinking and communication tool in the consulting industry.

**When to use it**:
- Writing management reports, proposals, and planning documents
- Situations where a complex claim must be built logically
- Designing the structure of meeting materials and presentation slides
- Outlining papers and reports

**Template**:
```
            [Claim (main message)]
           /        │        \
    [Ground 1]      [Ground 2]      [Ground 3]
    /    \      /    \      /    \
[Fact] [Fact] [Fact] [Fact] [Fact] [Fact]
```

| Level | Content | Checkpoint |
|-------|------|----------------|
| Claim | | Can it be stated in one sentence? Does it answer "So what?" |
| Ground 1 | | Does it directly support the claim? |
| └ Facts and data | | Are they verifiable facts? Is there a source? |
| Ground 2 | | Is it MECE with Ground 1? |
| └ Facts and data | | |
| Ground 3 | | Do the three grounds sufficiently support the claim? |
| └ Facts and data | | |

**Two approaches to building it**:
- **Top-down**: Decide the conclusion (claim) first, then repeat "Why?" to derive the grounds and facts. Suited to presentations and reports.
- **Bottom-up**: Gather facts and data, then repeat "So what?" to derive grounds and then the claim. Suited to research and analysis.

**MECE check**: Confirm that the grounds are organized "mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive". Typical ways to cut:
- Time axis: short term / medium term / long term
- Target axis: customer / company / competitor (3C)
- Process axis: planning / development / sales / support

### Worked example

**Situation**: Preparing a proposal for the board of directors that "we should strengthen our own e-commerce site"

```
                           the customer base]   competitive environment]

[EC gross    [Mall fees  [Customer    [Repeat rate  [Major mall  [Rise of D2C

 vs mall      per year]   in-house]    vs 22% on     in 3 years]   expanding

```

| Level | Content |
|-------|------|
| Claim | We should prioritize strengthening our own e-commerce site |
| Ground 1 | Profitability: our own e-commerce gross margin is 15 points higher than through malls |
| └ Facts | Own EC gross margin 45% / mall gross margin 30% / mall fees 200 million yen per year |
| Ground 2 | Customer base: with our own e-commerce site, we hold and use customer data directly |
| └ Facts | Own EC repeat rate 38% / mall repeat rate 22% / room to raise LTV through data use |
| Ground 3 | Competitive environment: growing risk of mall dependence and the need to respond to the D2C trend |
| └ Facts | Mall fees have doubled in 3 years / D2C brands' market share is expanding (15% annual growth) |

### Common mistakes

1. **Weak "So what?"**: The grounds are just listed, and the claim does not answer "So what?". Raise "Sales are growing" → So what? → to "Now is the right time to expand investment".
2. **Grounds are not MECE**: The grounds overlap (example: "Sales are growing" and "Customer count is growing" may overlap). Or an important perspective is missing. Deciding the way to cut first and then fitting in the facts makes it easier to be MECE.
3. **Uneven depth of hierarchy**: An imbalance such as five facts under Ground 1 but only one under Ground 3 shows weak logic. Support each ground at a similar level of detail.

### Tips

- **One slide = one pyramid**: Structure each presentation slide so that the heading is the main message and the body is the grounds and facts.
- **Be aware of induction and deduction**: If the grounds are arranged as "case A + case B + case C → common rule", that is induction; "major premise + minor premise → conclusion" is deduction. Induction is easier to use in business.
- **Choose grounds with objections in mind**: Anticipate the points where the listener may think "But what about...?" and include them in the grounds.
- **The rule of three**: As a rule, use three grounds. Two is thin, and four or more become scattered.

### Next steps

- At the stage of gathering data for the pyramid's grounds → **market** skill (PEST Analysis, Five Forces Analysis), **customer** skill (STP, Persona)
- To deliver the claim built with the pyramid concisely with PREP → **PREP** (this skill, 01)
- To build an execution plan for the claim → **execution** skill (OKR, Roadmap)
- To organize the way the grounds are cut into MECE → **problem** skill (MECE, Logic Tree)
- If an organizational decision-making framework is needed → **strategy** skill (Ansoff Growth Matrix, SWOT Analysis)

## 04. Elevator Pitch

**Purpose**: Convey the essence of a business or idea in 30 seconds to 1 minute. The name comes from catching the interest of an investor you happen to share an elevator with, in the short time before they get off. Best for situations where "the first impression decides the outcome", such as internal new business proposals, startup pitches, and first sales approaches.

**When to use it**:
- Pitches to investors and VCs
- Internal new business contests
- Introducing yourself at trade shows and conferences
- First sales meetings (the lead-in after the icebreaker)
- Introducing your company in hiring interviews

**Template**:
| Element | Content | Key point |
|------|------|---------|
| **Target customer** | For (whom) | A concrete persona. Not "small businesses" but "executives of IT companies with 50 or fewer employees" |
| **Problem** | (what) is the problem | A pain the customer feels daily. Quantified is even better |
| **Product name** | (product name) is | A memorable name. A working name is fine if there is none |
| **Category** | a (category) | Tie it to a category the listener already knows so they can understand it |
| **Key benefit** | that provides (main value) | Talk in benefits (value to the customer), not features |
| **Difference from competitors** | Unlike (competitor) | Name the single best-known competitor |
| **Unique differentiation** | it is characterized by (differentiator) | A unique strength competitors cannot easily copy |

**Completed sentence template**: "For ______, ______ is a problem. ______ is a ______ that provides ______. Unlike ______, it is characterized by ______."

### Worked example

**Situation**: Proposing an AI-powered automatic meeting minutes service at an internal new business contest

| Element | Content |
|------|------|
| **Target customer** | Managers at large companies who have 10 or more internal meetings per week |
| **Problem** | They spend 3 to 5 hours every week writing minutes and cannot focus on the decision-making work they should be doing |
| **Product name** | MeetingBrain |
| **Category** | AI meeting minutes and meeting assistant SaaS |
| **Key benefit** | Reduces minute-writing time by 95% through automatic recording, summarizing, and task extraction of meetings |
| **Difference from competitors** | Unlike existing transcription tools (Otter.ai and others) |
| **Unique differentiation** | It learns the company's internal business context (project names, internal terms, past decisions) and automatically extracts "who decided what and what should happen next" |

**Completed sentence**: "For managers who have 10 or more meetings per week, spending 3 to 5 hours every week writing minutes is a problem. MeetingBrain is an AI meeting minutes and meeting assistant SaaS that provides a 95% reduction in minute-writing time. Unlike existing transcription tools, it is characterized by learning the company's internal business context and automatically extracting decisions and next actions."

### Common mistakes

1. **Talking about features, not benefits**: Not "It transcribes with AI" (feature) but "It cuts minute-writing by 95%" (benefit). The listener wants to know the value to them, not the technology.
2. **Target customer is too broad**: "All business people" resonates with no one. Deliberately narrow to a niche at first. Making clear "who it is not for" actually increases persuasiveness.
3. **Weak or temporary differentiation**: "Nice UI" and "cheap" are weak differentiators. Talk about structural advantages competitors cannot catch up with in six months (proprietary data, network effects, technical patents, and so on).

### Tips

- **Test whether you can say it aloud in 30 seconds**: Actually say the completed sentence out loud and time it. If it exceeds 1 minute, cut.
- **The "So what" test**: Ask "So what?" about each element. Rewrite any element you cannot answer for.
- **Customize for the listener**: For investors, emphasize market size and scalability. For prospective customers, emphasize pain points and the solution. For internal senior management, emphasize ROI and synergy with existing businesses.
- **Use the "X for Y" analogy**: Comparing to a known service, such as "Shazam for businesses" or "Salesforce for restaurants", makes it instantly understood. However, do not rely on the analogy alone; always add the unique aspects.

### Next steps

- To go deeper on each element of the elevator pitch:
  - To deepen understanding of the target customer → **customer** skill (Persona, Customer Journey Map, STP)
  - To analyze the structure of the problem → **problem** skill (Logic Tree, Why-Why Analysis)
  - To analyze the differences from competitors systematically → **market** skill (Five Forces Analysis), **strategy** skill (SWOT Analysis, VRIO Analysis)
- To prepare detailed explanation materials for after the pitch → **Pyramid Structure** (this skill, 03) for the logical structure
- To design the whole business model → **strategy** skill (Business Model Canvas, Lean Canvas)
- To evaluate the feasibility of the idea → **idea** skill (Feasibility Study, Payoff Matrix)

## How to run the dialogue

### Phase 1: Interview (understand the situation)

Use AskUserQuestion to confirm the following:

1. **What you want to convey**: What do you want to communicate? (proposal, report, persuasion, introduction)
2. **Audience**: Who are you communicating to? (manager, executives, client, investors, team members)
3. **Situation**: What kind of situation is it? (meeting, email, presentation, sales meeting, Slack)
4. **Time constraint**: How many minutes or characters do you have?
5. **Audience's prior knowledge**: How much does the audience know about this topic?
6. **Expected output**: What form should the final output take? (slide structure, email text, spoken script)

**Interview tips**: Do not ask everything at once. Get the big picture with 1 or 2 questions first, then dig into details after selecting the framework.

### Phase 2: Framework selection and proposal

- Based on the interview results, propose the single most suitable framework
- Briefly explain why that framework is the best fit
- Where useful, also propose combinations such as "build the skeleton with PREP and reinforce the logic with Pyramid Structure"

### Phase 3: Fill in the template (interactively)

- Fill in each element of the template in dialogue with the user
- If the user's answer is vague, ask questions that prompt them to be concrete
  - "Could you be a bit more specific?"
  - "How much is that in numbers?"
  - "What is the first question the audience will have when they hear that?"
- At each step, check whether a "common mistake" applies, and point it out gently if it does

### Phase 4: Complete and output

- Format the completed framework in Markdown and output it
- Match the output format to the user's wishes:
  - **For presentations**: A structure proposal per slide (heading + body + notes)
  - **For email**: Subject line + finished body text
  - **For speaking**: A script converted into spoken language (with a time estimate)
  - **For reports**: Section structure + body text
- Finally, propose links to other frameworks as "next steps"

### Phase 5: Polish (optional)

- If the user wishes, provide feedback from the following perspectives:
  - **Logic check**: Do the grounds sufficiently support the claim? Are there any leaps?
  - **Audience check**: When read from the audience's position, are any questions or objections left unanswered?
  - **Impact check**: Does the opening sentence catch interest? Does the ending prompt action?
