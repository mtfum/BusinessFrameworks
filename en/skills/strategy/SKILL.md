## Framework selection guide

| What you want to do | Recommended framework | Supporting frameworks |
|---|---|---|
| Decide investment priority across multiple businesses | PPM | Core Competence Analysis |
| Decide the direction of growth | Ansoff Growth Matrix | Positioning Map |
| Find points of differentiation from competitors | Positioning Map | Blue Ocean Strategy, 4P Analysis |
| Create a new market | Blue Ocean Strategy | Positioning Map, BMC |
| Design a business model | BMC | Scheme Diagram, 4P Analysis |
| Validate a startup hypothesis | Lean Canvas | BMC, AIDMA |
| Organize marketing initiatives | 4P Analysis | AIDMA, Positioning Map |
| Design the customer purchase process | AIDMA | 4P Analysis |
| Clarify your company's strengths | Core Competence Analysis | Ansoff Growth Matrix |
| Explain how the business works to stakeholders | Scheme Diagram | BMC |

### Common combination patterns

- **New business planning**: Lean Canvas → BMC → Scheme Diagram (hypothesis → model design → visualization of the mechanism)
- **Turning around an existing business**: PPM → Core Competence Analysis → Ansoff Growth Matrix (understand the current state → identify strengths → decide growth direction)
- **Formulating a differentiation strategy**: Positioning Map → Blue Ocean Strategy → 4P Analysis (competitor analysis → differentiation design → concrete initiatives)
- **Marketing design**: 4P Analysis → AIDMA → Positioning Map (organize initiatives → design the purchase process → confirm differentiation)
- **Preparing an investor pitch**: Lean Canvas → Scheme Diagram → Core Competence Analysis (business overview → mechanism → competitive advantage)

## 01. PPM (Product Portfolio Management)

**Purpose**: Classify businesses and products on two axes, "market growth rate" and "relative market share", to optimize resource allocation

**Template**:
|  | Market share: high | Market share: low |
|---|-------------|-------------|
| **Growth rate: high** | ★ Star | ? Question Mark |
| **Growth rate: low** | 💰 Cash Cow | 🐕 Dog |

**Business placement**:
| Business | Market growth rate | Relative share | Quadrant | Investment policy |
|-------|----------|----------|------|---------|
| | | | | |

### When to use
- When a company with multiple businesses or product lines wants to decide where to concentrate limited management resources (budget, people)
- When reviewing the annual investment plan or business portfolio
- When you want to decide on investment in new businesses and withdrawal from existing businesses at the same time

### Worked example (general consumer electronics manufacturer)
| Business | Market growth rate | Relative share | Quadrant | Investment policy |
|-------|----------|----------|------|---------|
| Smart home business | 15% annual growth | 0.6 (2nd place) | Question Mark | Selective investment. Invest heavily if share growth is likely, otherwise consider withdrawal |
| Refrigerator business | 1% annual growth | 1.8 (1st place) | Cash Cow | Maintain the status quo. Reinvest profits in other businesses |
| EV charger business | 25% annual growth | 1.3 (1st place) | Star | Invest aggressively. Maintain and expand share |
| Fax business | -5% annual decline | 0.4 (3rd place) | Dog | Phased withdrawal. Harvest remaining profits |

### Common mistakes
- **Vague market definition**: If the market is defined too broadly, such as "IT business", growth rate and share become meaningless. Break business units down appropriately
- **Deciding mechanically by quadrant alone**: It is easy to jump to "Dog = withdraw immediately". A Dog may have synergies, and a Question Mark may not offer a return on investment
- **Stopping at a snapshot**: Beyond analyzing a single point in time, it is important to track changes over time (how businesses move between quadrants)

### Tips
- Set market growth rate objectively using industry data (government statistics, research firm reports, etc.)
- Calculate relative share as "your share / largest competitor's share". A value of 1.0 or more means high share
- Presenting it as a bubble chart with business size (revenue) as circle size makes decision-making in management meetings easier
- Adding a forecast of market growth rate 3 to 5 years out lets you make investment decisions ahead of time

### Next steps
- Dig deeper into the competitive environment of each business → **/market** (market analysis)
- Plan to invest Cash Cow profits in Question Marks → **/execution** (execution planning)
- Build consensus on withdrawal decisions → **/communication** (stakeholder explanation)
- Organization design after portfolio restructuring → **/organization** (organization design)

## 02. Ansoff Growth Matrix

**Purpose**: Organize growth strategy direction into four patterns by existing/new "market" × "product"

**Template**:
|  | Existing products | New products |
|---|--------|-------|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration strategy | New product development strategy |
| **New markets** | Market development strategy | Diversification strategy |

Concrete measures for each quadrant:
- Market penetration:
- New product development:
- Market development:
- Diversification:

### When to use
- When discussing "where to get the next growth" with executives
- When deciding the direction of a mid-term management plan (3 to 5 years)
- When growth of the existing business has slowed and you are looking for new growth drivers

### Worked example (online English conversation service)
|  | Existing product (online English conversation) | New products |
|---|--------|-------|
| **Existing market (working adults in Japan)** | Market penetration: strengthen referral campaigns, higher lesson frequency plans, expand corporate contracts | New product development: AI speaking practice app, TOEIC preparation course, business English proofreading service |
| **New markets** | Market development: expansion into South Korea and Taiwan, junior courses for elementary and junior high school students, curriculum provision for universities | Diversification: STEAM education combining programming and English, global talent training packages for companies |

### Common mistakes
- **Pursuing every quadrant**: Resources get spread thin and nothing is done well. The rule is to prioritize and focus on one or two quadrants
- **Neglecting market penetration**: New businesses attract attention, but digging deeper into existing markets × existing products is the lowest-risk option, and many companies still have room to grow there
- **Vague definition of "new"**: Difficulty differs greatly depending on whether a "new market" is truly unfamiliar to your company or an adjacent area. Proceeding without clarity leads to misjudged risk

### Tips
- State the risk level for each of the 4 quadrants (market penetration: low, new product development: medium, market development: medium, diversification: high)
- Listing the capabilities needed in each quadrant (technology, sales channels, brand, etc.) makes the feasibility discussion concrete
- Add a time axis and turn it into a roadmap, such as "market penetration in the short term, new product development in the mid term, diversification in the long term"
- Splitting diversification into "related diversification" and "unrelated diversification" gives finer-grained risk assessment

### Next steps
- Research the size and attractiveness of new markets → **/market** (market analysis)
- Generate new product ideas → **/idea** (idea generation)
- Check whether the direction makes use of your strengths → **/problem** (problem analysis) to understand the current state
- Execution plan for the chosen strategy → **/execution** (execution planning)

## 03. Positioning Map

**Purpose**: Visualize your position relative to competitors on a two-axis map to discover differentiation points and empty spaces

**Template**:
```
        Axis Y (high)
          ↑
    ●B    |    ●C
          |
──────────┼──────────→ Axis X (high)
          |
    ●A    |    ●Us
          |
        Axis Y (low)
```
- Axis X: (e.g., price, low → high)
- Axis Y: (e.g., features, few → many)
- Companies/products to plot: 

**How to use**: Choosing the axes is the key. Use the customers' key buying factors (KBF) as axes.

### When to use
- When deciding the position of a new product or service
- When you want to share differentiation points from competitors within the company
- When considering repositioning (brand redefinition) of an existing product
- When looking for empty spaces (positions with no competitors)

### Worked example (project management tool market)
```
      Feature richness (high)
          ↑
  ●Jira  |    ●Asana
          |
──────────┼──────────→ Price (high)
          |
  ●Trello |    ●Our tool
          |  (simple × fair price,
          |   focused on SMBs)
      Feature richness (low)
```
- Axis X: price (low → high)
- Axis Y: feature richness (few → many)
- **Finding**: There is an empty space in the "simple and fairly priced" zone. There is room to enter as the "tool you can actually master" that small and mid-sized businesses want

### Common mistakes
- **Choosing axes that favor your company**: The axes must be the factors customers weigh when buying (KBF), not the axes where your company looks good, or the map is meaningless
- **Correlated axes**: For example, "quality" and "price" are highly correlated, so plots just line up on a diagonal and yield little insight on differentiation. Choose axes that are highly independent
- **Stopping after plotting**: Do not be satisfied with making the map. The real point is to discuss "should we enter the empty space" and "why is it empty (no demand, or nobody has noticed)"

### Tips
- List 5 to 6 candidate axes, then narrow down to the "2 factors that most influence the purchase decision" through customer interviews or surveys
- Making several maps of the same market with different axes gives a multi-angle understanding of the competitive environment
- Making them over time reveals competitor moves and market trends (e.g., compare the map from 3 years ago with today's)
- Representing share or revenue by circle size adds information

### Next steps
- Validate needs in the empty space → **/customer** (customer analysis)
- Turn differentiation into concrete initiatives → **/execution** (execution planning)
- Consider blue ocean potential → "04. Blue Ocean Strategy" in this skill
- Detailed competitor analysis → **/market** (market analysis)

## 04. Blue Ocean Strategy (Strategy Canvas)

**Purpose**: Pursue differentiation and low cost at the same time to create an uncontested, untapped market

**Template (ERRC grid)**:
| Action | Factors | Concrete measures |
|----------|------|-------|
| **Eliminate** | | |
| **Reduce** | | |
| **Raise** | | |
| **Create** | | |

**Strategy canvas**: Compare the competitors' value curve with your new value curve

### When to use
- When you are worn down by price competition with rivals (escaping the red ocean)
- When you want to question industry norms and design a completely new value proposition
- When you want to create a product or service in a "category that has never existed"

### Worked example (budget fitness gym)
| Action | Factors | Concrete measures |
|----------|------|-------|
| **Eliminate** | Pool, studio classes, towel rental | Remove large facilities to cut initial investment and operating costs substantially |
| **Reduce** | Staffed hours, front desk service | IC card entry and exit for 24-hour unstaffed operation. Staff focus on cleaning and maintenance |
| **Raise** | Opening hours, number of machines | Open 24 hours, 365 days. Machine density that achieves zero waiting time |
| **Create** | 2,980 yen monthly price point, training log in the app | One third of the industry average price. App integration supports self-directed training |

**Strategy canvas (value curve comparison)**:
```
Value level
High│    ★        ★         ★
    │   / \      / \     ★ /
    │  /   \    /   \   / \/
    │ /     \  /     ★ /
 Low│/  ──────\/───────/──────
    └─────────────────────────→
     Price Facilities Staff Hours App Machines
    
    ── Conventional gym   ★ Budget gym (us)
```

### Common mistakes
- **Thinking only about Eliminate and Create**: Balance across all 4 actions, including Reduce and Raise, matters. Extreme cuts alone damage customer value
- **Ideating without the customer**: It is easy to decide "what to remove" from the industry's logic. Remove the things customers truly feel are unnecessary
- **Ending up with an easily copied strategy**: A blue ocean that only lowers prices is easy to imitate. Build in mechanisms that are structurally hard to copy (network effects, unique resources, etc.)

### Tips
- Always draw the strategy canvas with the "industry standard value curve" and "your value curve" side by side. The difference must be clearly visible
- Filling in the ERRC grid in an internal workshop with members from each department brings in diverse perspectives
- Focusing on "noncustomers" (people who currently do not use the industry's services) broadens Create ideas
- Summarize the finished ERRC as "in one sentence, what is new" and check whether it works as an elevator pitch

### Next steps
- Analyze noncustomers → **/customer** (customer analysis)
- Generate ideas for a new value proposition → **/idea** (idea generation)
- Translate into a business model → "05. BMC" in this skill
- Concrete execution roadmap → **/execution** (execution planning)

## 05. Business Model Canvas (BMC)

**Purpose**: Visualize the whole business model on one page using 9 blocks

**Template**:
| Block | Content |
|---------|-----|
| **KP** Key Partners | |
| **KA** Key Activities | |
| **KR** Key Resources | |
| **VP** Value Propositions | |
| **CR** Customer Relationships | |
| **CH** Channels | |
| **CS** Customer Segments | |
| **C$** Cost Structure | |
| **R$** Revenue Streams | |

**How to use**: Fill in VP (Value Propositions) and CS (Customer Segments) first, then expand the other blocks from there.

### When to use
- When designing the business model for a new business
- When you want to visualize an existing business model and find improvement points
- When explaining the whole business to investors or prospective partners
- When comparing multiple business model options

### Worked example (flea market app)
| Block | Content |
|---------|-----|
| **KP** Key Partners | Delivery companies (Yamato Transport, Japan Post), payment providers (credit card companies), identity verification services |
| **KA** Key Activities | Platform development and operation, fraud monitoring, customer support, marketing |
| **KR** Key Resources | App and system infrastructure, user base (network effects), brand trust, AI price suggestion engine |
| **VP** Value Propositions | Sellers: easily turn unwanted items into cash. Buyers: get what they want cheaper than new. Anonymous shipping for safe transactions |
| **CR** Customer Relationships | Self-service (completed in-app), trust building through a rating system, chat support |
| **CH** Channels | Smartphone app (iOS/Android), TV commercials and social media ads, word of mouth |
| **CS** Customer Segments | Sellers: women in their 20s to 40s (disposing of fashion and baby items). Buyers: a broad range of value-conscious users |
| **C$** Cost Structure | Server and infrastructure costs, development payroll, marketing costs such as TV commercials, customer support operations |
| **R$** Revenue Streams | Sales commission (10% of sales), shipping fee margin, sale of promotional slots (future) |

### Common mistakes
- **Vague VP (Value Propositions)**: Ending with an abstract phrase like "a convenient service". Write concretely which customer problem is solved and how
- **No consistency across the 9 blocks**: Check the connections between blocks, such as whether what is listed in KR (resources) is used in KA (activities), or whether CH (channels) reaches CS (customer segments)
- **Low resolution on costs and revenue**: If C$ and R$ are filled in "roughly", you cannot verify whether the business is viable. Go into concrete amounts for the main cost items and revenue sources

### Tips
- Spend the first 30 minutes only on VP and CS. If these two are not settled, the other blocks drift
- Identify "which of the 9 blocks carries the highest risk (uncertainty)" and start hypothesis testing there
- Creating and comparing competitors' BMCs clarifies your differentiation points and weaknesses
- Even for one business, create multiple BMCs if customer segments differ (e.g., B2B and B2C)

### Next steps
- Detailed design of the value proposition → **/customer** (dig into jobs, pains, and gains in customer analysis)
- Diagram how the business works → "10. Scheme Diagram" in this skill
- Run hypothesis testing fast → "06. Lean Canvas" in this skill
- Make marketing initiatives concrete → "07. 4P Analysis" in this skill

## 06. Lean Canvas

**Purpose**: A simplified BMC for startups. Run the hypothesis testing cycle fast

**Template**:
| Block | Content |
|---------|-----|
| **Problem** (Top 3) | |
| **Customer Segments** | |
| **Unique Value Proposition** | |
| **Solution** | |
| **Channels** | |
| **Revenue Streams** | |
| **Cost Structure** | |
| **Key Metrics** | |
| **Unfair Advantage** | |

**How to use**: The difference from BMC is the inclusion of "Problem", "Solution", "Key Metrics", and "Unfair Advantage". Use it at the stage of high uncertainty.

### When to use
- When first organizing a new business or startup idea
- At the hypothesis stage before verifying "is this really what customers want?"
- When considering a pivot (change of business direction)
- When building the skeleton of an investor pitch deck

### Worked example (AI chat SaaS for automating corporate customer support)
| Block | Content |
|---------|-----|
| **Problem** (Top 3) | 1. CS costs account for 30% of personnel expenses 2. Cannot handle inquiries at night or on holidays 3. Response quality varies by operator |
| **Customer Segments** | E-commerce businesses (500+ inquiries per month), CS teams at SaaS companies |
| **Unique Value Proposition** | Learns your FAQ within 30 minutes of setup and auto-answers 70% of inquiries. Human operators can focus on complex cases |
| **Solution** | AI chatbot fine-tuned on your own data. Check and improve answer accuracy from the admin console. Includes escalation to human agents |
| **Channels** | Content marketing (case study articles), listings on SaaS comparison sites, trade shows, webinars for CS managers |
| **Revenue Streams** | Monthly SaaS fee (usage-based by inquiry volume, 50,000 to 300,000 yen per month), onboarding support fee (100,000 yen initial) |
| **Cost Structure** | AI infrastructure (GPU/API), development team payroll (5 people), marketing, customer success payroll |
| **Key Metrics** | Auto-response rate, customer satisfaction (CSAT), MRR (monthly recurring revenue), churn rate, onboarding lead time |
| **Unfair Advantage** | Proprietary industry-specific training dataset (pre-trained on 100,000 inquiry records from the e-commerce and SaaS domains) |

### Common mistakes
- **Problem and Solution do not match**: Check that the solution actually solves the 3 problems listed. A mismatch sends hypothesis testing in the wrong direction
- **Writing generic strengths as the "Unfair Advantage"**: "Strong technology" or "an excellent team" is not an advantage. Write things that cannot be copied easily (network effects, proprietary data, patents, brand)
- **Not setting key metrics**: If it is unclear what counts as "working", you cannot decide whether to pivot

### Tips
- Fill it in within 20 minutes the first time. Do not aim for perfection. Write the current "best hypothesis"
- A Lean Canvas is not fixed as a single sheet. Update it after every round of hypothesis testing and keep versions (v1, v2, v3...)
- "Problem" is a hypothesis before customer interviews. Always revise it after the interviews
- Keep the balance between the right side (customer segments, channels, revenue) and the left side (problem, solution, cost) in mind

### Next steps
- Validate the problems → **/customer** (customer interview design)
- Validate a solution prototype → **/idea** (make ideas concrete)
- Scale after reaching PMF → **/execution** (execution planning)
- Refine the model based on validation results → "05. BMC" in this skill
- Improvement cycle for numeric targets → **/improvement** (improvement and retrospectives)

## 07. 4P Analysis (Marketing Mix)

**Purpose**: Design marketing initiatives across the 4 elements of Product, Price, Place, and Promotion

**Template**:
| 4P | Us | Competitor A | Competitor B |
|----|-----|------|------|
| **Product** (product/service) | | | |
| **Price** (price/fee structure) | | | |
| **Place** (distribution/sales channels) | | | |
| **Promotion** (sales promotion/communication) | | | |

**How to use**: The 4Ps are the seller's view. Contrasting them with the customer-side 4Cs (Customer Value, Cost, Convenience, Communication) deepens the analysis.

### When to use
- When organizing the full picture of marketing initiatives at a new product launch
- When comparing initiatives with competitors to understand your strengths and weaknesses
- When considering how to allocate the marketing budget
- When identifying the cause of declining sales of an existing product

### Worked example (coffee chain)
| 4P | Us | Starbucks | Convenience store coffee |
|----|-----|------|------|
| **Product** | Hand-dripped single-origin beans. Roast date shown. Food in partnership with a local bakery | Extensive customization, seasonal limited Frappuccinos, wide food lineup | Freshly brewed coffee for 100 yen. Few varieties but stable quality |
| **Price** | Drip 450 yen, latte 550 yen (mid-range, but quality justifies it) | Drip 390 yen, latte from 490 yen. Customization raises the ticket | Drip 100 yen, latte 150 yen. Overwhelmingly low price |
| **Place** | 3 street-level stores within a 3-minute walk of a station. Small takeout-focused stores | Over 1,800 stores nationwide. Shopping centers and near stations | 55,000 convenience stores nationwide. Overwhelming number of locations |
| **Promotion** | Instagram (stories about the growing regions), flyers to nearby offices, stamp cards | TV commercials, social media, rewards program, UGC | In-store POP, tie-ins with TV commercials. Impulse buys during store visits |

**Reframing from the 4C perspective**:
| 4P | 4C (customer perspective) | What it means to our customers |
|----|---|---|
| Product | Customer Value | "The reassurance and sense of occasion of knowing who grew the beans and where" |
| Price | Cost | "A bit expensive for every day, but just right as a twice-weekly treat" |
| Place | Convenience | "It is near the office, so I can stop by on the way to work" |
| Promotion | Communication | "Seeing the stories about the growing regions on Instagram makes me want to support them" |

### Common mistakes
- **Treating the 4Ps separately**: Inconsistency across the 4Ps, such as a high-quality Product with a low Price, confuses customers
- **Thinking of Place only as stores**: Design to include digital channels such as e-commerce sites, social media, apps, and subscriptions
- **Leaning on Promotion**: When sales stall, the reflex is to add advertising, but often the Product itself needs rethinking or the Price strategy needs to change

### Tips
- Define the Product thoroughly first, then design the other Ps. If the product is vague, everything else drifts
- When filling in competitors' 4Ps, actually buying and using their products raises the resolution
- Always attach "why we do it this way (what it means to the customer)" to each 4P item
- The emphasis differs between B2B and B2C. In B2B, Place and Product carry more weight. In B2C, Promotion has a larger effect

### Next steps
- Execution schedule for each initiative → **/execution** (execution planning)
- Dig deeper into target customers → **/customer** (customer analysis)
- Design initiatives along the purchase process → "08. AIDMA" in this skill
- Visualize differentiation from competitors → "03. Positioning Map" in this skill

## 08. AIDMA

**Purpose**: Model the consumer's purchase psychology as a 5-stage process and design initiatives for each stage

**Template**:
| Stage | Customer state | Initiatives | KPI |
|------|----------|------|-----|
| **A**ttention | Unaware → aware | | |
| **I**nterest | Aware → interested | | |
| **D**esire | Interested → wants it | | |
| **M**emory | Wants it → remembers it | | |
| **A**ction | Remembers it → buys | | |

**Note**: In the digital era, also consider derivative models such as AISAS (Search, Share) and AISCEAS.

### When to use
- When designing the path from awareness to purchase for a new product
- When identifying where in the funnel the problem lies, such as "awareness exists but purchases do not follow"
- When organizing marketing initiatives along the customer journey
- When building the rationale for allocating budget across advertising, PR, and sales promotion

### Worked example (new product launch for an organic cosmetics brand)
| Stage | Customer state | Initiatives | KPI |
|------|----------|------|-----|
| **A**ttention | Unaware → aware | Instagram ads (addressing skin concerns), PR posts by beauty influencers, press release distribution | Ad impressions: 5 million/month, media mentions: 20 |
| **I**nterest | Aware → interested | Brand story video on ingredient commitment, landing page (before and after cases), free sample application page | Landing page visits: 50,000/month, sample applications: 3,000 |
| **D**esire | Interested → wants it | Earn high ratings on review sites, publish dermatologist-supervised evidence, 30% off first-purchase coupon | Review rating: 4.2 or higher, coupons issued: 2,000 |
| **M**emory | Wants it → remembers it | Retargeting ads, weekly updates via the official LINE account, email newsletter (skincare tips) | LINE friends: 5,000, newsletter open rate: 25% |
| **A**ction | Remembers it → buys | E-commerce exclusive bundles, simplified purchase flow (3-step checkout), points for posting reviews | Monthly e-commerce sales: 5 million yen, purchase conversion rate: 3%, review posting rate: 15% |

### Common mistakes
- **Concentrating too much budget on Attention (awareness)**: If awareness exists but Desire and Action initiatives are weak, customers drop out midway through the funnel. Invest in a balanced way across stages
- **Neglecting the Memory stage**: Even when customers want the product, they often forget it before the moment of purchase. Reminder initiatives are unglamorous but important
- **Designing offline and online separately**: Real customers research online and buy in store (or the reverse). Cross-channel design is needed

### Tips
- First get the current funnel data in numbers (awareness rate → interest rate → purchase rate), and concentrate investment on the stage with the biggest drop
- For digital products, AISAS (Attention → Interest → Search → Action → Share) often fits reality better
- Limit each stage's KPIs to "1 main metric + 1 or 2 sub-metrics". Too many metrics cannot be tracked
- Extending to post-purchase "Share" and "Repeat" lets you also design initiatives to raise LTV (customer lifetime value)

### Next steps
- Break each stage's initiatives into concrete tasks → **/execution** (execution planning)
- Refine the target customer persona → **/customer** (customer analysis)
- Measure and improve initiative effectiveness → **/improvement** (improvement and retrospectives)
- Overall design of promotion initiatives → "07. 4P Analysis" in this skill

## 09. Core Competence Analysis

**Purpose**: Identify your company's core capabilities and build a strategy around them

**Template**:
| Candidate capability | Contribution to customer value | Differentiation from competitors | Transferability | Assessment |
|-------------|--------------|------------|----------|------|
| | High/Medium/Low | High/Medium/Low | High/Medium/Low | |

**3 conditions**:
1. Provides value that customers recognize
2. Hard for competitors to imitate
3. Can be applied across multiple markets and products

### When to use
- When the management team wants to put into words "what our real strengths are"
- When deciding the direction of diversification or M&A based on your strengths
- When identifying "capabilities to protect" during reorganization or restructuring
- When defining the source of competitive advantage that anchors the mid-term management plan

### Worked example (precision equipment manufacturer)
| Candidate capability | Contribution to customer value | Differentiation from competitors | Transferability | Assessment |
|-------------|--------------|------------|----------|------|
| Nano-level precision machining technology | High (directly enables smaller, higher-performance products) | High (20 years of accumulation, 50 patents) | High (applicable to medical devices, semiconductors, automotive) | ★ Core competence |
| Global sales network | Medium (improves customer convenience) | Medium (major competitors have comparable networks) | Medium (horizontal expansion through existing channels is possible) | Important but not core |
| Rapid after-sales service system | High (directly supports uptime guarantees) | Medium (differentiating but imitable) | Low (depends on the equipment being serviced) | A strength but not core |
| In-house integrated production (design → manufacturing → inspection) | High (quality stability) | High (competitors relying on outsourcing lack it) | Medium (applicable to new products too) | Semi-core competence |

**Conclusion**: "Nano-level precision machining technology" is the core competence. The growth strategy should center on this technology and prioritize expansion into the medical device market and the semiconductor equipment market.

### Common mistakes
- **Confusing "strengths" with "core competence"**: Sales capability, brand, location, and the like are often "strengths" but do not meet the 3 conditions of a core competence. Only what meets all 3 conditions is a core competence
- **Looking only at current capabilities**: Also consider "emerging capabilities" that are weak now but could become core competences with investment
- **Not sharing across the organization**: Analysis by executives alone is meaningless if it does not reach the front line. It is important that the whole company agrees "this is our core competence"

### Tips
- List at least 5 to 8 candidates before narrowing down by the 3 conditions. Narrowing from the start limits your view
- Test contribution to customer value with the question "if this capability did not exist, would customers switch to another company?"
- Evaluate inimitability quantitatively with "how many years and how much money would it take to copy this capability?"
- Core competences become obsolete over time. Reassess them every 3 to 5 years

### Next steps
- Growth strategy built on the core competence → "02. Ansoff Growth Matrix" in this skill
- Analyze target markets for expansion → **/market** (market analysis)
- Organization design that supports the core competence → **/organization** (organization design)
- Execution plan for strengthening capabilities → **/execution** (execution planning)

## 10. Scheme Diagram

**Purpose**: Diagram how the business works (flows of people, goods, money, and information) to visualize the structure among stakeholders

**Template**:
```
[Player A] ──service provision──→ [Player B]
      ↑                            │
      │                            ↓
   Payment (fee)              Payment (usage fee)
      │                            │
      └─────── [Player C] ←──────┘
```

**Elements to include**: Actors (stakeholders), value provided, flow of money, flow of information, contractual relationships

### When to use
- When explaining the business model to stakeholders (investors, partners, internal members)
- When organizing the structure of a model with multiple stakeholders, such as a platform business
- When you want to include an "overall diagram of the mechanism" in a new business proposal or business plan
- When you want to clarify contractual relationships and scope of responsibility

### Worked example (crowdsourced translation platform)
```
                    [Translators (individuals)]
                    /        ↑
           Translated work     Payment (80% of translation fee)
                  /            ↑
                ↓              │
[Client company] ──translation request──→ [Our platform] ←──registration and skill certification
```

### Common mistakes
- **Vague arrow direction or meaning**: Always label what each arrow represents (money, service, information, contract). A diagram with unlabeled arrows is read differently by each reader
- **Missing stakeholders**: Regulators, indirect beneficiaries, end users, and others are easy to overlook. List stakeholders exhaustively before drawing
- **Making it too complex**: Cramming all information into one diagram makes it unreadable. Split into an overview version (main flows only) and a detailed version (all flows)

### Tips
- Write stakeholders on sticky notes and arrange them physically before drawing arrows. This makes organizing easier
- Color-coding "flow of goods", "flow of money", and "flow of information" makes the structure clear at a glance
- The most important point in a scheme diagram is "where your company sits and what it earns revenue from"
- Showing parts that may change in the future (e.g., shift to direct transactions, removal of intermediaries) with dotted lines makes strategic discussion easier

## How to run the conversation

### Basic flow

1. Use AskUserQuestion to hear about the business and the strategic issue
2. Refer to the framework selection guide (table above) and propose the most suitable framework
3. Fill in the template interactively
4. Output the completed worksheet in Markdown
5. Propose related frameworks or connections to other skills as next steps

### Points to confirm during the hearing

| Item | Example question |
|---------|-------|
| Business phase | "Is this a new launch or an improvement of an existing business?" |
| Type of issue | "Is it closest to growth strategy, differentiation, marketing, or business model design?" |
| Use of the output | "Is this for internal discussion or for investors?" |
| Time horizon | "Which do you prioritize: short term (up to 1 year), mid term (1 to 3 years), or long term (3+ years)?" |
| Existing analysis | "Do you have any analyses already done (SWOT, 3C, market research, etc.)?" |

### How to combine frameworks

A single framework rarely completes the job. Combining them in the following order works well.

**Pattern 1: New business from scratch**
```
/problem (problem discovery) → /customer (customer understanding) → Lean Canvas → BMC → Scheme Diagram → 4P Analysis → AIDMA → /execution (execution)
```

**Pattern 2: Strengthening competitiveness of an existing business**
```
/market (market analysis) → Core Competence Analysis → Positioning Map → Blue Ocean Strategy → 4P Analysis → /execution (execution)
```

**Pattern 3: Restructuring the business portfolio**
```
PPM → Core Competence Analysis → Ansoff Growth Matrix → BMC (each business) → /organization (reorganization)
```

**Pattern 4: Overhauling the marketing strategy**
```
/customer (customer analysis) → Positioning Map → 4P Analysis → AIDMA → /execution (execution) → /improvement (measure effectiveness and improve)
```
